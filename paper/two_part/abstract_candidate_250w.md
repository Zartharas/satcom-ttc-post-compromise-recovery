After a key compromise, ground and spacecraft can end a recovery attempt holding different keys, leaving operators unable to command the spacecraft or commanding on state they have not confirmed. Fresh key establishment does not settle this when intermittent contact, message loss and endpoint restarts interrupt activation and verification. We study this recovery layer in two parts, with abstract cryptography and no link measurements. Part I is a deterministic fault-injection harness for a resynchronization controller and three baseline abstractions. Four of 31 single-fault cells converged without command or telemetry evidence, but only because the controller attempts verification once by design; a later run giving it three transmissions removed those outcomes, leaving nothing that separates it from a baseline with retransmission under loss. Retransmission does not rebuild destroyed endpoint state, and where the spacecraft has already activated the new key the ground is locked out. These follow from the model's rules. Baseline parity is weak (two of four families inject no fault), and a 100-schedule mixed population reached only 77 of 191 scheduled faults, so it is descriptive. Part II computes a six-message activation-and-verification exchange exactly, with hypotheses declared by the author beforehand. Retries alone could not clear indeterminate outcomes; resumable verification, an assumption outside the standards, could; bursty loss changed outcomes in both directions; and several expected monotonicities failed. Assuming a static recovery association, 83-90% of 6,960 divergent points reached a 0.95 recovery level, depending on retry policy and that threshold. Neither part claims CCSDS conformance.
