Fresh key establishment alone does not restore trusted satellite telemetry, tracking, and command operation after compromise when intermittent contact, message loss, and endpoint state loss leave ground and spacecraft in different states. We study this recovery layer in two parts. Part I uses a deterministic fault-injection model with a resynchronization controller and three baseline abstractions. Its findings: converged keys are not verified recovery (four of 31 single-fault cells ended indeterminate for lack of command or telemetry evidence); retries absorbed single drops but not lost verification evidence, which the controller attempts once by design, or destroyed endpoint state; and endpoint restarts, like exhausted retries, left a compromised key active (two cells and all eight retry-exhaustion schedules). Baselines matched the controller only in four families, two without faults, and a later grid showed retransmission alone absorbs isolated loss. A 100-schedule mixed population reached only 77 of 191 scheduled faults, so it is descriptive. Part II computes a six-message activation-and-verification exchange exactly, with hypotheses frozen beforehand: plain retries could not clear indeterminate outcomes, resumable verification, an assumption outside the standards, could; bursty loss changed outcomes in both directions; several expected monotonicities failed; and, assuming a static recovery association, recovery reached the 0.95 level in 83-90% of 6,960 divergent points, by retry policy. These are abstraction properties, not link measurements, and neither part claims CCSDS conformance. The results support separating candidate state, activation, verification evidence, and persistent recovery state.
