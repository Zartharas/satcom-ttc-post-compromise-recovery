After a key compromise, ground and spacecraft can end a recovery attempt holding different keys, leaving operators unable to command the spacecraft or commanding on state they have not confirmed. Fresh key establishment does not settle this when intermittent contact, message loss and restarts interrupt activation and verification. We study this recovery layer in a reproducible, software-only harness that reports alignment, security state, availability and verification evidence separately, with abstract cryptography and no link measurements. Part I injects faults into a resynchronization controller and three baselines. Converged keys were not verified recovery: four of 31 single-fault cells converged without command or telemetry evidence. Retransmission absorbed lost messages but not destroyed endpoint state, and a spacecraft that had activated the new key locked the ground out. A later post hoc run giving the controller three transmissions removed its indeterminate outcomes, so it is not separated from a baseline with retransmission; these findings follow from the model's rules. Baseline matching is weak (two of four families inject no fault) and the 100-schedule population reached only 77 of 191 scheduled faults, so it is descriptive. Part II computes a six-message activation-and-verification exchange exactly, with hypotheses declared by the author beforehand. Repeating expired attempts alone could not clear indeterminate outcomes; resumable verification, an assumption outside the standards, could; bursty loss changed outcomes in both directions; and several expected monotonicities failed. Assuming a static recovery association, 83-90% of 6,960 divergent points reached a 0.95 recovery level, depending on policy. Neither part claims CCSDS conformance.
